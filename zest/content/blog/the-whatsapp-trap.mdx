A tutor in Lagos has 47 unread WhatsApp messages. Half are parents asking "are you available Thursday?" The other half are follow-ups from sessions that already happened. This is what "running a business on WhatsApp" actually looks like.

It's not working. But nobody wants to admit it, because WhatsApp feels like the most natural way to run a business in Nigeria. It's free. Everyone has it. It's instant. So coaches, tutors, therapists, and photographers started using it for everything — booking, payments, scheduling, follow-ups. It made sense at first.

Then the business grew, and WhatsApp became the problem.

## What the trap looks like

A parent messages you at 9pm: "Can my daughter do Saturdays?" You see it but you're cooking. You think "I'll reply later." Later becomes tomorrow morning. By then, they've messaged two other tutors. You reply "Yes, Saturday works!" They say "Oh, we found someone else." That's a client lost to a slow reply.

Or this: a client asks "are you free Friday afternoon?" You say "yes" because your calendar looks open. An hour later, another client messages: "Friday 2pm still good?" You check — wait, you just gave that slot away. Now you're double-booked. Or you're cancelling on someone.

Or the worst one: a client you haven't heard from in months messages "hi." You scroll back through 6 months of chat history trying to remember who they are, what they booked last time, and whether they still owe you money. The chat is buried under grocery lists and family group messages. You give up.

These aren't unusual. This is how most service businesses in Nigeria operate every day.

## How we got here

WhatsApp didn't set out to be a business tool. It's a messaging app. It was built for conversation — friends, family, groups. But in Nigeria, it became the default for everything because nothing else was as accessible.

No email accounts to set up. No apps to download. No accounts to create. Just open WhatsApp, find the number, send a message. For a tutor getting their first clients, or a photographer handling inquiries, it's the fastest path from "I need clients" to "I have clients."

And when you're doing 3 or 4 bookings a week, it works fine. The back-and-forth is manageable. You remember who everyone is. You can keep track in your head.

The trap springs when the business grows. At 10 bookings a week, the WhatsApp back-and-forth takes hours. At 20, it's unmanageable. Messages get lost. Clients get forgotten. You start losing people — not because you're bad at your job, but because you can't keep up with the chat.

## The real cost

Let me try to put a number on this. Say you spend 15 minutes per client on WhatsApp back-and-forth — checking availability, confirming times, answering the same questions you answered last time. For 20 clients a week, that's 5 hours. Five hours of typing "yes, Thursday 4pm works" and "let me check my schedule."

That's not just time lost. It's clients lost. Every time you take 3 hours to reply to a booking inquiry, there's a chance that person books someone else. Every time you double-book because you forgot a slot was taken, you lose trust. Every time a client has to repeat their background because you can't find their last conversation, you look unprofessional.

And here's the quieter cost: you can't grow. If every new client means more WhatsApp messages to manage, you hit a ceiling. Not because you can't handle the work — because you can't handle the chat.

## WhatsApp is the answer and the problem

I don't think the solution is to quit WhatsApp. That would be absurd. Your clients are there. They trust it. They respond faster on WhatsApp than they ever would on email.

The problem isn't WhatsApp. The problem is using WhatsApp for things it wasn't built for.

WhatsApp is great for: quick questions, confirmations, follow-ups, sending a photo, saying "I'm running 10 minutes late."

WhatsApp is terrible for: scheduling, managing availability, tracking who booked when, keeping client records, accepting payments.

Using WhatsApp for bookings is like using your phone's calculator to do your taxes. It technically works. But it's not the right tool, and it gets painful fast.

## The hybrid approach

The service providers who figure this out don't abandon WhatsApp. They just stop using it for everything.

Here's what it looks like: you share a booking link on WhatsApp. "Hey! You can book directly here: zestbook.org.ng/book/adecoach." The client opens it, picks a time, answers your questions, and they're done. You get a notification on WhatsApp. No back-and-forth.

WhatsApp becomes what it's good at — a communication channel. The booking page handles what WhatsApp can't — availability, scheduling, records.

The parent who messaged at 9pm? They get a link, book at 9:05pm, and you wake up to a confirmed session. No lost client.

## A real example

Before: A photographer gets a DM. "How much for a photoshoot?" She replies with packages. "Can you do next Saturday?" She checks her calendar. "Yes, morning works." "What time?" "10am or 12pm." "12pm works. Where's your studio?" She sends the address. "Okay, see you then." Seven messages over two days. The client might show up. They might not.

After: Same photographer shares a booking link on WhatsApp status. Client opens it, sees packages and prices, picks Saturday 12pm, fills in their name and phone number, gets a confirmation. Zero messages. The photographer just gets a notification: "New booking: Saturday 12pm."

The first version wastes 20 minutes and risks losing the client to a slow reply. The second version takes zero minutes and locks in the booking.

## What this isn't

I'll be honest: if you're doing 3 or 4 bookings a week and the WhatsApp back-and-forth takes 10 minutes total, you don't have a problem. WhatsApp is fine for that. The trap bites when the volume grows — when you're spending hours a week on scheduling chat instead of doing the work.

And a booking page doesn't replace WhatsApp. It doesn't replace the personal touch of messaging a client to confirm, or the trust you build through conversation. It just removes the scheduling part from the chat so you can focus WhatsApp on what it does best — talking to people.

## The bigger idea

I think WhatsApp is the best communication tool in Nigeria and the worst business tool. The distinction matters. Service providers who use WhatsApp for everything hit a ceiling. The ones who use WhatsApp for conversation and a booking page for scheduling — those are the ones who grow.
