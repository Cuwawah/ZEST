There's a version of this business that makes more sense on paper, and I want to argue against it.

Most software treats the free tier as a billboard — a small, deliberately inconvenient version of the product that exists to make the paid version look attractive. The free plan is not useful. It is a demonstration of insufficiency.

I've run several products built that way. It works, in the way that a locked door works.

The uncomfortable thing I noticed while building Zest is that the free plan is the part that has to be genuinely good. Everything else is downstream of it. And once I believed that, I couldn't work out what I was charging ₦4,000 a month for.

I'm writing this down because it's an argument I now hold but can't yet prove, and I'd rather be honest about which half is which.

**On data:** I don't have a conversion number. I don't know what share of free accounts ever pay, and I haven't measured whether making the free plan better increases revenue at all — it might not. I can tell you what the software does. I can't tell you yet whether the strategy is correct.

## The free tier is the only thing anyone evaluates

Before anyone pays you anything, they use the free version. That's the entire sample. Not a demo of the free version — the free version.

This is where most products lose people, and it's rarely the pricing. It's that the free version is awkward in some small way that makes the product feel unfinished. You can't add a second thing. The branding is theirs, not yours. There's a limit, and the limit is hit in the first ten minutes.

None of those are feature decisions. They're tone decisions. And they cost you the evaluation.

For Zest, the free plan is one event type, with a week of Pro on the house. A tutor who needs tutoring — one recurring slot, a form, a confirmation — can run their entire business on it, indefinitely, without paying us a naira. That's a real product, not a trial.

The only reason I can promise that is that I was forced to make the free path work before the paid path did.

## The strongest objection

The obvious objection is that this argument devalues what people actually pay for. If the free plan does the job, you've told me the thing I bought is the throwaway part. That's backwards, and it makes me less likely to upgrade, not more.

I don't think it's backwards, but I want to take the objection seriously rather than deflect it, because the honest version of it is: *your free tier is a privilege, not a strategy, and you're describing it as a strategy to feel better about your pricing.*

That would be fair if the free plan were a giveaway. It isn't. It's a floor. The question is whether you raise the floor or build a wall, and for anyone with one event type, the honest answer is that there is no wall to build.

## What Pro actually removes

Here's what I think ₦4,000 a month buys, and it is a shorter list than most pricing pages imply.

It removes friction, not capability. Unlimited event types, so a coach running separate offers isn't rationing them. Your branding instead of mine, because your clients are seeing your name and not a badge. Priority support when something breaks on a Friday.

The person who upgrades is not buying the ability to run their business. They could already do that. They're buying the removal of small, constant annoyances — and the annoyances only start mattering once you care about the business enough to notice them.

That's a narrower product than "everything, but better," and I think narrow is honest.

## The change that convinced me

The clearest evidence I have isn't a metric. It's a bug.

When someone's subscription expired, the account used to lock. Booking stopped. You logged in and found a dead end.

We changed it. An expired subscription now drops you to the free plan. One event type, but live, still taking bookings, no lockout.

A locked account is a cancellation with extra steps. Someone locked out on a Tuesday doesn't come back on Wednesday, they just conclude the software stopped working. Someone dropped to free is a user who might upgrade, and might not, and is definitely still sending you referrals.

Nobody pays for that decision. I think a lot of people are retained by it.

## Pro is friction, not features

If I had to compress the whole argument into a line, it would be that **Pro is friction, not features.**

The features are the product. The friction is what you charge for. If you're selling features, you're competing with every free thing, and you will lose, because there is a version of what you built available to someone for nothing on a site that already has the traffic.

If you're selling the removal of annoyance, you can compete on whether the annoyance is real, which is a much more honest fight.

## What this isn't

This isn't an argument for giving everything away. Somebody has to pay for the hosting and the bank transfers, and ₦4,000 is a number I set with very little information behind it. I may be wrong about the price.

It also isn't a claim that I've found the right model. I've changed the free plan and I'm watching what happens. It is entirely possible that the correct move is a smaller free tier and a higher price, and that "the free plan is the real product" is just a nice story I tell myself about a pricing decision I haven't earned yet.

And the obvious failure mode is real: build a free plan so generous that nobody ever needs to pay, and congratulations, you've built a charity.

## The bigger thing

I think this is downstream of something larger about software in this market.

A product that asks someone to pay before it has proven anything is asking for trust in advance. In a market where the alternatives are a WhatsApp thread that works fine for three bookings a month, or a foreign tool that costs more than the service being sold, the burden of proof sits with the person charging — not the person being sold to.

The free plan is how you discharge that burden. It's not generosity. It's the argument for the charge.

So the thing I'm actually selling at ₦4,000 is not unlimited event types. It's the removal of the risk that the free thing stops working on you at exactly the moment your business got busy enough to care.

That's a small promise. I'd rather make a small one I can keep.
