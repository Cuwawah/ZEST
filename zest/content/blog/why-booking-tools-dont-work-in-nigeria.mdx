Every week, thousands of Nigerian coaches, tutors, therapists, and photographers lose hours to the same conversation:

*"Are you available on Thursday?"*
*"Thursday morning works, what time?"*
*"Can you do 4pm instead?"*
*"Let me check..."*

This conversation happens 20 times a week for a busy service provider. That's 20 times they could have lost a client to someone who replied faster. It's not a feature problem. It's a structural problem — the tools that were supposed to fix this were built for a different economy.

## The imported tool problem

Calendly, Acuity, Square Appointments — these are excellent products. They were built for markets where:

- Clients pay with credit cards
- Communication happens over email
- Bookings are cold — the client found you online, they don't know you yet
- The transaction is the relationship

Nigerian service businesses don't operate this way. The actual infrastructure looks like:

- **WhatsApp is the business.** Not email. Not SMS. WhatsApp. Every client communication starts and ends there.
- **Bank transfer is the payment rail.** Most clients don't have credit cards. They open their banking app, transfer the money, send a screenshot. That's the entire payment flow.
- **Relationships drive revenue.** The first booking is the cheapest part. The real value is the second, third, tenth booking — the parent who books tutoring every term, the couple who comes back for annual photos, the client who renews coaching every quarter.

Calendly solves scheduling. It doesn't solve the fact that your client paid by transfer and you need to match that payment to a name. It doesn't solve the fact that your best client hasn't booked in 6 weeks and you should probably reach out. It doesn't solve the fact that 67% of your clients never come back, and you don't know why.

These aren't missing features. They're different problems entirely.

## The moment vs. the relationship

Here's the thing I keep coming back to: most booking tools capture the **moment** — the 30 seconds where a client picks a time slot. But the business isn't the moment. The business is what happens before and after.

Before: does this client know me? Have they booked before? What did they tell me last time? What questions should I ask before we meet?

After: did they come back? How often do they book? When was the last time? Are they drifting away?

No scheduling tool captures this. They're all built around the calendar grid — available slots, booked slots, buffer times. That's useful, but it's solving the least interesting part of the problem.

## What "client memory" actually means

I've been thinking about this as **client memory** — the idea that your booking system should know who your clients are, not just when they're available.

A scheduling tool remembers time. Client memory remembers:

- That Adaeze has booked 7 times and is your most loyal client
- That Chukwuemeka hasn't booked in 45 days and you should reach out
- That every parent who asks about "JAMB prep" also asks about pricing before the first session
- That your busiest month is September and you should prepare extra slots
- That 3 of your last 5 first-time clients never rebooked — maybe your intake form is filtering them out

This is the stuff that actually grows a service business. Not a prettier calendar. Not another notification. The intelligence to know who matters and when to act.

## The limitation

I'll be honest: this isn't for everyone. If you're running a high-volume transactional business — a chain of salons with 50 stylists and walk-in clients — Zest isn't built for that. It's for the individual provider or small practice doing 10-50 bookings a month, where each client relationship matters and losing one person to a slow reply actually hurts.

And the client intelligence is early. It tracks patterns and surfaces insights, but it's not a full CRM. It won't send automated follow-up emails or build marketing funnels. It does one thing: helps you know your clients better so you can serve them better. If that's what you need, it works. If you need a Swiss Army knife, there are better tools.

## The bigger idea

I think there's a whole category of software that was imported into African markets without being rebuilt for how those markets actually work. Booking tools are one example. Invoicing, client management, payments — there's a long list of tools that work great in San Francisco and fall apart in Lagos, not because the engineering is bad, but because the assumptions are wrong.

The assumption that clients pay with cards. The assumption that email is the primary channel. The assumption that every booking is a cold transaction. The assumption that the tool's job ends when the slot is booked.

I don't think the answer is "build Calendly but African." I think the answer is start from the relationship — the WhatsApp thread, the bank transfer, the returning client who's been with you for two years — and build outward from there.

That's what I'm trying to do with Zest. Not a scheduling tool. A system for remembering your clients.
