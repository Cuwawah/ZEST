# Why Every Therapist and Counsellor Needs Intake Forms

The first session with a new client is one of the most important moments in a therapeutic relationship. It sets the tone, builds trust, and determines whether that person will come back. But too often, therapists spend the entire session asking basic questions — "Why are you here?" "Have you seen a therapist before?" "Is there anything you'd like me to know?" — and run out of time before doing any real work.

It doesn't have to be that way. A simple intake form sent before the first session can change everything.

## What intake forms actually do

An intake form is a short questionnaire you send to a client before they walk through your door or log into a video call. It covers the basics: why they're seeking therapy, relevant background, any medications, and practical details like emergency contacts.

The value isn't just logistical. Intake forms do three things at once:

**They prepare you.** Instead of spending the first twenty minutes gathering information, you already have a clear picture of what this person needs. You can walk into the session with a plan, ready to go deeper from the start.

**They set a professional tone.** In a field where credibility matters, having a structured onboarding process signals that you take your practice seriously. Clients notice when things are organised. It reassures them they're in good hands.

**They make sessions better.** When you're not scrambling to understand a client's history in real time, you can actually listen. You can focus on what they're saying between the lines. You can do the work you trained to do.

## What to ask before the first session

You don't need a twenty-page document. A good intake form is short — five to eight questions, enough to give you context without overwhelming the client. Here are the questions that matter most:

1. **What brought you here today?** This gives you the client's own words about their primary concern. It's more useful than you think — people often frame their issues differently than a referral might.

2. **What are you hoping to get out of therapy?** Goals matter. This tells you whether the client wants coping strategies, self-exploration, help with a specific relationship, or something else entirely.

3. **Have you been to therapy before?** A client's previous experience shapes their expectations. If they had a bad experience, you'll want to know. If therapy is new to them, you can adjust your approach.

4. **Are you currently taking any medication?** This is clinical information you need before session. It also helps you understand the full picture of their mental health care.

5. **Is there anything you'd prefer not to discuss in the first session?** This question alone sets you apart. It gives the client a sense of control and shows you respect their boundaries.

6. **How did you hear about us?** Referral source matters. Whether it's Google, a friend, or a doctor's recommendation, it tells you what's working in your outreach and helps you tailor your introduction.

7. **Do you have any accessibility needs or preferences for session format?** This covers everything from physical accessibility to video call preferences. It's a small detail that makes a big difference.

8. **Emergency contact details.** Standard practice, but often overlooked until it's too late. Get it upfront.

## How to deliver intake forms

Here's where most therapists get it wrong. They draft a Word document, attach it to an email, and hope the client remembers to fill it out before showing up. The result? Clients arrive having forgotten about the form, or they fill it out in the waiting room five minutes before session.

Email attachments are clunky. They're easy to miss, hard to track, and they don't integrate with anything else in your workflow.

The better approach is embedding your intake form directly into your booking process. When a client books a session, the form is right there — part of the same flow. They fill it out once, it's saved, and you have everything you need before they arrive.

## What changes after you start using forms

Therapists who adopt intake forms usually notice three shifts within the first few weeks.

**Onboarding gets faster.** You stop repeating the same introductory questions with every client. Sessions start where they should — with the client's actual concerns.

**No-shows go down.** When a client has already invested time filling out a form and committing to their goals, they're less likely to cancel last minute. The form creates a sense of commitment before session one even happens.

**The client experience improves.** Clients feel seen and understood from the start. They don't have to re-explain their story on day one. They walk into a session where the therapist is already prepared, already listening, already engaged.

This is what professionalism looks like in practice. Not a fancy office or expensive branding — just a simple system that respects everyone's time and energy.
